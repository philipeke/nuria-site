Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq, An-Nas: three surahs that protect you

Nuria · Jul 30, 2026 · 3 min read

Three surahs, one complete shelter

Towards the end of the Quran, tucked among the shorter surahs, sit three chapters that scholars across all four madhhabs have described as a complete spiritual shield. Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq, and An-Nas—together known as al-Muwwidhatain (the two seeking-refuge surahs) alongside Al-Ikhlas—are among the most recited verses in a Muslim's day, and for good reason.

The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have recited each of these three surahs three times after Fajr and three times after Maghrib, and to have blown gently into his cupped hands and wiped over his body before sleeping. This is preserved in hadith collections agreed upon by scholars as authentic, and it remains one of the most accessible and meaningful adhkar a Muslim can hold on to.

What each surah is really saying

Al-Ikhlas: the surah of pure oneness

Al-Ikhlas is four ayat that stand as a complete theological statement. Qul huwa Allahu Ahad—Say: He is Allah, the One. It affirms that Allah is Al-Samad, the eternally self-sufficient upon whom all creation depends, that He does not beget and was not begotten, and that there is nothing comparable to Him.

The Prophet ﷺ said this surah is equal in reward to a third of the Quran. Scholars have understood this to mean it covers the essential attribute of Tawhid—the oneness of Allah—which is the foundation upon which all of Islam rests. Reciting it is not just a habit; it is a repeated renewal of the most central conviction in a believer's heart.

Al-Falaq: seeking refuge from what we cannot see

Al-Falaq is a dua woven into a surah. We ask refuge with the Lord of the daybreak—Rabb al-Falaq—from the evil of what He has created, from the darkness as it settles, from those who blow on knots (a reference to harmful magic), and from the envy of the envious.

These are real concerns that the Quran takes seriously. Envy exists. Harm can come from unseen sources. The surah does not encourage fear or obsession with these realities—it offers the correct response to them: turning to Allah, acknowledging His lordship over everything that was created, and placing yourself under His protection.

An-Nas: seeking refuge in the Lord of all people

An-Nas completes the trio by directing our attention inward. We seek refuge with the Rabb, Malik, and Ilah of humankind—the Lord, the Sovereign, and the God of people—from the whispering of the retreating one, who whispers into the chests of people, whether from jinn or from people.

The greatest threat named here is the whisper—al-waswas—the subtle, internal suggestion that erodes conviction, fuels doubt, and pulls a person away from remembrance. This surah is a recognition that the struggle is often interior, and that the one who protects you from it is the same one who created you.

How to make these surahs a living practice

  • After Fajr and Maghrib: Recite each surah three times. This is an established Sunnah and takes less than two minutes.
  • Before sleeping: Recite each surah once (or three times), blow into your cupped hands, and wipe over your face and as much of your body as you can reach. The Prophet ﷺ did this consistently even in illness.
  • As a grounding practice: When anxiety rises, or when something feels unsettled, returning to these three surahs is not superstition—it is placing your trust exactly where it belongs.

You do not need to be a scholar to benefit from these surahs. You need only recite them with presence, understanding what they mean, and genuine reliance on the One you are turning to. That is enough. That has always been enough.
