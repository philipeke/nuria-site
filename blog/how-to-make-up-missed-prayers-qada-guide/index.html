How to make up missed prayers (qada): a clear guide

Nuria · Aug 7, 2026 · 3 min read

Life interrupts us. Sleep overtakes us. There are periods—sometimes long ones—where prayers were missed, skipped, or never learned. If you carry that weight, know that the door to making things right is open. Qada is how we return.

What is qada?

Qada (قضاء) means making up a prayer that was missed after its prescribed time has passed. It is distinct from ada, which is performing a prayer within its time. Scholars across all four major madhhabs—Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali—agree that obligatory prayers (fard) missed without a valid permanent excuse must be made up. This is a settled point of Islamic jurisprudence.

When does qada apply?

You owe qada for a prayer when it was missed due to:

  • Oversleeping—the Prophet ﷺ himself missed Fajr on one occasion while travelling and made it up upon waking
  • Forgetfulness—the hadith is clear: "Whoever forgets a prayer, let him pray it when he remembers it"
  • Ignorance of Islam—many new Muslims and returning Muslims carry years of missed prayers; scholars have addressed this with compassion
  • Illness or incapacity—where prayers were missed entirely (not merely shortened or combined)

Prayers are not owed qada for a woman during her menstrual period or postnatal bleeding (nifas)—this is a mercy and a relief, not an oversight.

How to perform qada prayers

The method is straightforward: pray the missed prayer as you would have prayed it at its time, including the same number of rak'ahs and the same obligatory components. A few practical points:

  • Intention (niyyah) matters. Inwardly intend that you are making up a specific missed prayer—"I am praying the qada of Fajr from such-and-such a day" is enough; you don't need to say it aloud
  • No set order is required according to most scholars, though the Hanafi school recommends making up prayers in order if the number is small (six or fewer)
  • You may pray qada at any time except during the three universally prohibited times: at sunrise, at the sun's zenith (exact midday), and at sunset—though some schools distinguish between forbidden and disliked times for qada specifically
  • Sunnah prayers are not owed qada as an obligation, though you may make them up if you wish

What if you have many years of missed prayers?

This is the question that silences many people—and it shouldn't. Scholars across the madhhabs have addressed this with nuance and mercy.

The general principle: begin. Make a sincere intention to complete your qada, and start praying your five daily prayers consistently alongside making up what you can. Even one qada prayer a day is progress. There is no requirement to calculate an exact number; consistency and sincere effort are what matter.

Some contemporary scholars advise performing additional voluntary prayers (nafl) alongside regular qada to cover gaps where the count is uncertain. Others encourage a steady, sustainable pace—one extra prayer after each obligatory prayer, for example. Consult a scholar you trust for guidance specific to your madhhab and situation.

A note on compassion

Allah knows what was in your heart during every period of your life. Returning to prayer—and returning to make up what was missed—is itself a profound act of tawbah (repentance) and connection. The Prophet ﷺ said: "When one of you sleeps through a prayer or forgets it, let him pray it when he remembers. There is no expiation for it other than that."

Begin where you are. That is enough.
