How to make up missed prayers (qada): a clear guide

Nuria · Aug 4, 2026 · 3 min read

Life happens. Illness, travel, forgetfulness, or—for those who came to the deen later in life—years spent before Islam. Whatever the reason, missing prayers is something many Muslims carry quietly. The good news is that Islam is a religion of mercy, and the door to making up what was missed is wide open.

What is qada?

Qada (قضاء) simply means making up a prayer after its prescribed time has passed. It is distinct from ada, which is performing a prayer on time. Scholars across all four madhhabs agree that missed obligatory prayers must be made up—this is a point of scholarly consensus.

Why prayers might be missed

The scholars distinguish between two situations:

  • Excused absence—sleeping through a prayer or genuinely forgetting it. The Prophet ﷺ said: Whoever sleeps through a prayer or forgets it, let him pray it when he remembers it. (Bukhari, Muslim)
  • Unexcused absence—missing a prayer deliberately. All four madhhabs hold that these prayers must still be made up, and the person should also seek sincere tawbah (repentance).

How to make up missed prayers

The method is straightforward: you pray each missed salah exactly as you would pray it at its time, including the same number of rakahs and the same recitations. There is no shortened version for qada.

Order of making up prayers

According to the Hanafi, Maliki, and Hanbali madhhabs, it is obligatory to make up prayers in the order they were missed—praying Fajr before Dhuhr, for example. The Shafi'i madhhab considers this order recommended but not strictly obligatory. If you have only a few missed prayers, maintaining order is straightforward. If you have a large number, scholars counsel beginning and being consistent, without becoming so rigid that it discourages you from starting.

When to make up missed prayers

You may make up missed prayers at almost any time—day or night. The exception is the three disliked times: at the moment of sunrise, at midday when the sun is at its zenith, and at the moment of sunset. These brief windows aside, qada prayers can be performed freely.

Intention for qada

When making your intention (niyyah), specify that this is a qada prayer. For example: I intend to pray the missed Fajr of such a day. You do not need to say this aloud—the intention is in the heart.

What about a lifetime of missed prayers?

For those who accepted Islam as adults, or who were away from the deen for many years, the idea of making up years of prayers can feel overwhelming. Scholars hold different views on this scenario, and you should consult a scholar of your madhhab for personalised guidance. What every scholar agrees on: begin, be sincere, and do not let the magnitude of the task become a reason not to start. Allah is Al-Ghafur, the Most Forgiving.

A word on consistency

Many people find it helpful to add a few qada prayers each day—perhaps one or two after Fajr, or before sleeping. The key is regularity over intensity. A small, steady practice will carry you further than an unsustainable burst of effort.

May Allah make it easy for you, accept your prayers, and fill your heart with peace as you return to what was missed. The fact that you are asking is itself a sign of a heart that cares.
