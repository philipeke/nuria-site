What is Witr prayer and when to pray it

Nuria · Aug 3, 2026 · 3 min read

What is Witr prayer?

Witr literally means odd number, and the prayer takes its name from the fact that it is always prayed in an odd number of units—one, three, five, seven, or more. It is among the most emphasized of all voluntary prayers. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ never abandoned Witr, whether he was travelling or at home, and he urged the community to observe it consistently.

There is a difference of opinion among scholars about Witr's ruling, and this is worth understanding so you can approach your own practice with confidence rather than confusion.

Is Witr obligatory or voluntary?

The Hanafi madhhab holds that Witr is wajib—obligatory in a strong sense, just below fard. Leaving it intentionally is a serious matter in the Hanafi view, and a missed Witr must be made up.
The Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali madhhabs consider Witr a confirmed Sunnah (Sunnah Mu'akkadah)—highly recommended, one of the most important voluntary acts, but not strictly obligatory. Regardless of which position you follow, the scholars are unanimous that Witr deserves your consistent attention.

When is the time for Witr?

The time for Witr begins after the 'Isha prayer and ends at the break of dawn (Fajr). Within that window, scholars have discussed the optimal time:

  • Before sleeping—If you are not confident you will wake for Tahajjud, pray Witr before you sleep. The Prophet ﷺ advised this for those who fear they might not rise.
  • The last third of the night—If you do regularly wake for night prayer, it is preferable to delay Witr until after Tahajjud and make it the seal of your night worship. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Make Witr your last prayer of the night."

If the time passes and you did not pray Witr, you may make it up after Fajr according to the Hanafi position, and some scholars in other madhhabs also permit this as a valued make-up, though opinions differ on the details.

How many rak'ahs is Witr?

The minimum is one rak'ah, though praying three, five, or more is more complete. The most common practice is three rak'ahs. The Hanafi school performs three rak'ahs with one final sitting (tashahhud), similar in structure to Maghrib. The other madhhabs typically pray two rak'ahs followed by one separate rak'ah, with a tasleem after the two. Both approaches are valid within their traditions—you follow the method of your madhhab or of a scholar you trust.

The Qunut supplication

In the Witr prayer—particularly in the final rak'ah—many Muslims recite du'a al-Qunut, a supplication asking Allah for guidance, forgiveness, and protection. The Hanafi school makes this part of every Witr throughout the year. The Shafi'i school recites it during the second half of Ramadan, while the Hanbali school permits it year-round. The Maliki school does not generally include it in Witr outside of Ramadan Qiyam.

A gentle reminder

Whatever your madhhab, Witr is a gift—a moment at the edge of the night or the beginning of sleep to turn back to Allah with your heart open. The Prophet ﷺ described it as something Allah loves. Let that be your motivation, more than any technical detail.
