Making dhikr a daily habit: why small moments add up

Nuria · Jul 25, 2026 · 4 min read

There is a moment, somewhere between putting down your phone and picking up your keys, when the day feels like it belongs entirely to the world. The commute, the meeting, the errand—each demand pulling you a little further from the quiet place inside you. Dhikr is what brings you back.

What dhikr actually means

The word dhikr literally means remembrance or mention. In its Islamic sense, it refers to any act of bringing Allah to mind—whether through the tongue, the heart, or both together. It encompasses the short phrases that the Prophet ﷺ recommended for specific times of day, as well as longer forms of praise, glorification, and supplication.

What makes dhikr remarkable is its accessibility. It requires no ritual purity, no specific place, and no fixed duration. It can happen while washing dishes, waiting at a traffic light, or sitting in the quiet before sleep.

The Quranic invitation

Allah says in the Quran:

"Remember Me; I will remember you." (Al-Baqarah 2:152)

This is one of the most direct and intimate promises in the Quran. It is not conditional on scholarship, eloquence, or a particular spiritual state. It simply asks for remembrance—and promises the same in return.

In another verse, Allah describes the people of understanding as those who remember Him standing, sitting, and lying on their sides (Al-Imran 3:191). The postures cover every waking state. The message is clear: dhikr is not meant to be confined to a corner of the day.

The science of small habits

Modern research on habit formation confirms what Islamic tradition has long practised: small, consistent actions reshape the mind more reliably than occasional grand efforts. The Prophet ﷺ himself said, in a hadith recorded by Bukhari and Muslim:

"The most beloved deeds to Allah are those done consistently, even if they are small."

This principle is not just spiritual wisdom—it describes how the human mind actually works. Repetition creates neural pathways. What begins as a deliberate act becomes, over time, a natural reflex. The person who says SubhanAllah thirty-three times each morning does not need willpower after the first few weeks. It simply becomes part of who they are.

Practical anchors for daily dhikr

One of the most effective ways to build a dhikr practice is to attach it to existing habits—what some call "habit stacking." Here are a few anchors that many Muslims find natural:

  • After each prayer: The tasbihat after salah—thirty-three SubhanAllah, thirty-three Alhamdulillah, thirty-three Allahu Akbar—take less than two minutes and are among the most well-documented practices of the Prophet ﷺ.
  • In the morning and evening: The prophetic morning and evening adhkar are a collection of specific phrases recommended at these times. Even three or four of them, said with presence, are better than rushing through the full list without attention.
  • During transitions: Leaving and entering the home, starting a meal, beginning a journey—each has a short dua or dhikr attached to it in the Sunnah. These moments of transition are easy to miss and easy to reclaim.
  • Before sleep: Ayat al-Kursi, the last two verses of Al-Baqarah, and the sleeping adhkar are well-loved for a reason. They settle the mind and close the day in remembrance.

Presence over quantity

There is a common worry that dhikr said by habit becomes hollow—a recitation of sounds without meaning. This concern is worth taking seriously, but the solution is not to abandon consistency. It is to practise presence alongside repetition.

Scholars across the madhhabs have noted that dhikr with the heart is higher in rank than dhikr of the tongue alone—but dhikr of the tongue with an absent heart is still better than silence. The tongue can lead the heart back. Often, mid-phrase, something shifts: a word that has been said a thousand times lands differently. That is the door opening.

The practice is to keep showing up, even on the days when the words feel like going through the motions. Consistency is what keeps the door in front of you.

A word about tools

Many Muslims today use a digital tasbih or guided dhikr reminders to help maintain their practice. There is nothing wrong with using tools that serve the deen. What matters is that the tool stays in its place—a reminder and support, not a substitute for the intention and presence that make dhikr what it is.

The goal is not to complete a number. It is to spend more of your day in the company of Allah.

Starting today

If dhikr feels far away right now, begin with one phrase. Just one. SubhanAllah—Glory be to Allah. Say it ten times, deliberately, after Fajr. Do it tomorrow. Do it the day after. Let the practice grow from there, without pressure and without performance.

The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever says SubhanAllah wa bihamdihi a hundred times during the day, his sins are wiped away, even if they are like the foam of the sea." (Bukhari and Muslim)

That is the mercy in this practice. The entry point is always small. The reward is always generous.
