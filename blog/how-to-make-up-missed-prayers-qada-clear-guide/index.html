How to make up missed prayers (qada): a clear guide

Nuria · Aug 2, 2026 · 3 min read

Life happens. A deep sleep, an illness, a moment of forgetfulness—and a prayer passes without you. If that's you, you're not alone, and the door to making it right is always open.

Making up a missed prayer is called qada (قضاء). It is an act of accountability and sincerity—returning to Allah what was due, even if the time has passed.

What counts as a missed prayer?

A prayer is considered missed when its prescribed time window has elapsed without it being performed. This applies to the five daily prayers: Fajr, Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib, and Isha.

Scholars across all four madhhabs—Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali—agree that qada is obligatory for prayers missed due to sleep or forgetfulness. The Prophet ﷺ said:

"Whoever forgets a prayer, or sleeps through it, let him pray it when he remembers it. There is no expiation for it other than that." (Bukhari & Muslim)

Is qada required for deliberately missed prayers?

This is where scholarly opinion differs, and it is worth understanding nuance here.

  • Hanafi, Maliki, and Shafi'i positions hold that qada is required even for prayers deliberately abandoned—though deliberate abandonment is a serious sin in itself.
  • Hanbali scholars hold a range of views; some hold that a deliberately missed prayer cannot be made up with qada in the same way, and that sincere repentance is the primary path. However, the predominant Hanbali position also recommends performing qada alongside repentance.

Whatever your madhhab, the unanimous counsel is: repent sincerely, return to prayer, and make up what you missed.

How to perform qada prayers

Qada prayers are prayed exactly as the original prayer would have been—the same number of rak'ahs, the same structure. There is no shortened or simplified version.

  • Make intention (niyyah) for the specific missed prayer—for example, "I am praying qada for Fajr of yesterday."
  • Pray it in full, as you normally would.
  • If you have many missed prayers, work through them gradually. Consistency matters more than speed.

What about the order?

The Hanafi and Maliki schools emphasize making up missed prayers in order (tartib), praying older ones before newer ones, provided the number of missed prayers is not overwhelming. When there are many, the obligation of order is generally lifted, and you pray what you can when you can.

The Shafi'i and Hanbali schools consider ordering recommended but not strictly obligatory.

Can you pray qada at any time?

Yes—with one important note. Most scholars agree that qada prayers may be prayed even during the three generally restricted times (sunrise, midday zenith, sunset), if you are making up a specific missed prayer rather than praying a voluntary one. The Hanafi school, however, maintains restrictions at these times more strictly—consult a scholar if you are unsure based on your madhhab.

A note on sincerity

Qada is not just a legal transaction—it is a conversation with Allah. When you sit down to make up a prayer, you are saying: I came back. That matters deeply.

Start where you are. Even one qada prayer today is a step. Allah is Al-Ghaffar—the Ever-Forgiving—and He knows your circumstances better than anyone.
