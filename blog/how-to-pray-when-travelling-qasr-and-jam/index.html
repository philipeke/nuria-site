How to pray when travelling: qasr and jam'

Nuria · Jul 31, 2026 · 3 min read

One of the most beautiful mercies in Islam is that the deen travels with you. Whether you're on a long flight, a road trip, or a journey far from home, your prayers remain—but Allah has granted ease in how you perform them. This ease comes through two concessions: qasr (shortening) and jam' (combining).

What is qasr?

Qasr means shortening the four-unit (raka'at) prayers to two. This applies to Dhuhr, Asr, and Isha. Fajr (two raka'at) and Maghrib (three raka'at) are not shortened—they remain as they are.

All four major madhhabs agree that qasr is valid for the travelling Muslim. Where they differ is in whether it is obligatory or preferred:

  • Hanafi: Qasr is obligatory for the traveller—performing four raka'at when travelling is disliked (makruh) unless you intend to stay for fifteen days or more.
  • Maliki and Shafi'i: Qasr is a confirmed Sunnah (Sunnah mu'akkadah) and the stronger option, though completing the prayer is valid.
  • Hanbali: Qasr is better than completing the prayer, and some scholars in this school view it as obligatory.

What is jam'?

Jam' means combining two prayers in one time slot. You may combine Dhuhr with Asr, or Maghrib with Isha. Fajr is never combined with another prayer.

There are two types:

  • Jam' taqdim: Advancing the later prayer into the earlier prayer's time—praying Asr during Dhuhr time, for example.
  • Jam' ta'khir: Delaying the earlier prayer to be prayed in the later prayer's time—praying Dhuhr during Asr time.

Again, the madhhabs differ:

  • Hanafi: Jam' for travellers is not permitted in the general sense. The school permits something called jam' al-suri—praying Dhuhr at the very end of its time and Asr at the very beginning of its time, which gives the appearance of combining without technically doing so.
  • Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali: Jam' is permissible for travellers, provided the journey meets the distance threshold.

How far is a travel distance?

The minimum journey distance that triggers the traveller's concessions is a point of scholarly discussion. The most widely cited threshold is approximately 80–90 kilometres (around 48–54 miles), based on the classical measurement of four burud. Check the position of your own madhhab if you follow one closely—the Hanafi school, for instance, uses a slightly different calculation.

When do the concessions end?

You remain a traveller—and may use these concessions—until you intend to settle at your destination for a period that ends the journey status. The duration that ends the concessions varies by madhhab:

  • Hanafi: Fifteen days or more of intended stay ends traveller status.
  • Maliki: Four days (excluding arrival and departure) is the threshold.
  • Shafi'i: Four days of intended stay ends the concession.
  • Hanbali: More than twenty prayers (approximately four days) is the general position.

A practical reminder

If you are praying behind an imam who is a resident (not a traveller), complete the prayer in full—you do not shorten in congregation behind a non-travelling imam. If you are leading others who are residents, the majority position is that you complete the prayer in full as well.
