How to read Quran with tajweed as a beginner

Nuria · Aug 1, 2026 · 3 min read

What is tajweed and why does it matter?

Tajweed comes from the Arabic root meaning to make something excellent. In the context of Quran recitation, it refers to the set of rules that govern how each letter and word is pronounced—including qualities of individual letters, rules of elongation (madd), assimilation (idgham), and stops.

The scholars across all four madhhabs agree that learning tajweed at a functional level is an individual obligation (fard 'ayn) for every Muslim who recites Quran. Reciting without any care for pronunciation is disliked, and in some cases can change meaning. But do not let this create anxiety—Allah is the Most Merciful, and the Prophet ﷺ told us that the one who struggles with recitation receives a double reward.

Start with the Arabic alphabet

Before learning tajweed rules, you need to be comfortable producing the sounds of Arabic letters. Several of these sounds do not exist in English or other languages—letters like ع (ayn), غ (ghayn), ح (ha), and خ (kha) require deliberate practice to pronounce correctly.

  • Use a Qaida (beginner recitation primer) to learn letter forms and basic sounds.
  • Listen carefully to how a qualified teacher or clear audio recording produces each letter.
  • Repeat each sound slowly—correct articulation from the outset saves you from unlearning bad habits later.

Learn the articulation points (makhaarij)

Every Arabic letter emerges from a specific point in the mouth or throat. These are called makhaarij al-huruf (articulation points). There are seventeen commonly cited points grouped across the throat, the tongue, the lips, and the nasal cavity.

You do not need to memorise all seventeen on day one. Begin with the letters you find most difficult. A good teacher will help you feel and hear the difference between, for example, the heavy ص and the light س, or the throat-deep ع and أ.

Find a teacher—even online

The Quran has always been transmitted orally, from teacher to student in an unbroken chain back to the Prophet ﷺ. No book or app fully replaces this. A teacher can hear your mistakes, correct your tongue, and give you the ijazah (certification) that confirms your recitation meets the standard.

If in-person lessons are not available to you, qualified online teachers are widely accessible today. Look for someone with a recognised chain of transmission in one of the established recitations—Hafs 'an 'Asim is the most widely taught worldwide, though all ten recitations are valid.

A practical daily routine

  • Listen first: Before reciting a new passage yourself, listen to a clear recording several times. Let your ear absorb the rhythm and sounds.
  • Recite slowly: Speed is not a virtue at this stage. Slow, deliberate recitation embeds correct habits.
  • Short sessions, consistently: Fifteen focused minutes every day builds more than a two-hour session once a week.
  • Review what you know: Spend part of each session on passages you have already worked through—revision cements retention.

Be patient with yourself

Learning tajweed is a lifelong journey—scholars who have spent decades studying still describe themselves as students. What matters is sincere effort and steady progress. Every surah you recite with care, every letter you work to pronounce correctly, is an act of worship. Begin where you are, ask Allah to open your heart and tongue, and take it one letter at a time.
