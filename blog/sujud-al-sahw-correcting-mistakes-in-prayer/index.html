Sujud al-sahw: correcting mistakes in prayer

Nuria · Jul 31, 2026 · 3 min read

We have all been there—midway through salah, a quiet panic: Did I pray two rak'ahs or three? Did I recite al-Fatiha in that unit? The heart quickens, and the tranquillity of the moment flickers. This is precisely the situation that sujud al-sahw—the prostration of forgetfulness—was designed for. It is one of the most practical mercies built into Islamic worship, a reminder that our Lord does not demand perfection, only sincerity and a willingness to correct ourselves.

What is sujud al-sahw?

Sujud al-sahw consists of two extra prostrations performed when a Muslim makes an error or omission in the obligatory elements of salah. The Prophet × said: "When one of you forgets, let him perform two prostrations." (Muslim). These two prostrations serve as a remedy for three types of situations:

  • Excess (ziyadah)—adding an extra rak'ah, rukoo', or sujood unintentionally
  • Deficiency (naqs)—omitting a wajib (obligatory) element, such as the first tashahhud
  • Doubt (shakk)—being genuinely uncertain about the number of rak'ahs performed

When does it apply—and when does it not?

Not every imperfection in prayer triggers sujud al-sahw. Minor slips in the sunnah acts—a slight pause, a quietly mispronounced word—generally do not require it. It becomes relevant when an obligatory (wajib) act is missed or a structural element is disrupted. A forgotten fard (pillar), such as a missed rukoo', is a more serious matter and in most schools must be made up within the prayer itself, not merely compensated by sujud al-sahw alone.

How it is performed: a general guide

The four main madhhabs agree that sujud al-sahw involves two prostrations with the tasleem, though they differ on when these prostrations occur and which tasleem precedes or follows them. Here is a broad overview:

Position of sujud al-sahw

  • Hanafi: Always performed after the first tasleem (to the right), followed by tashahhud and then the final tasleem.
  • Maliki: Generally performed before the tasleem for cases of deficiency; after for excess. Doubt is treated as deficiency.
  • Shafi'i: Always performed before the final tasleem, in both cases of excess and deficiency.
  • Hanbali: Generally performed after the tasleem, with some exceptions for cases of deficiency where it may come before.

The basic steps (Hanafi method as an example)

  • Complete your tashahhud and give the tasleem to the right only.
  • Perform two full prostrations (as in regular prayer).
  • Sit again, recite the tashahhud, and give both tasleems to conclude.

If you follow a different madhhab, apply the method of your school—all four are valid paths, and the underlying intention and humility are the same.

Handling doubt about rak'ahs

If you genuinely cannot remember whether you have prayed two or three rak'ahs, the general principle across schools is to build on certainty—assume the lesser number and complete the prayer accordingly. In the Hanafi school, one should act on the preponderant likelihood (ghalabat al-zann) if they have a strong feeling; otherwise assume the lesser. Sujud al-sahw is then performed after the prayer.

A word of reassurance

Sujud al-sahw is not a sign of failure—it is a sign of attentiveness. The Prophet himself performed it, teaching us that forgetfulness is human and that Islam provides a graceful way through it. If you are unsure of the ruling in a specific situation, it is always best to ask a knowledgeable person in your community or consult a scholar from your madhhab.
